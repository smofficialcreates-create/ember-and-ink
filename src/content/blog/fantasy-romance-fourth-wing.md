*Fourth Wing* sold more copies in its first year than most fantasy series sell in a decade. That kind of success invites two equally lazy responses — "it's just TikTok hype" or "it's the best romantasy of the decade". Both are wrong.

It's a very good book with a very specific kind of magic: it makes the *slow burn* feel earned, in a genre that is mostly allergic to earning it.

Here's a spoiler-light look at what it gets right — and where it strains.

## The setup (no spoilers)

Violet Sorrengail was meant to be a scribe. Instead, her mother — a general — forces her into the dragon rider quadrant at Basgiath War College, where cadets die in training as a matter of routine. Violet is small, chronically ill, and, by her own admission, unlikely to survive the first week.

Her greatest threat is not the dragons. It's Xaden Riorson, the wing leader whose father was executed by Violet's mother. He has openly sworn to kill her.

The rest, obviously, is history.

## Why the romance works

Most romantasy does not actually write a slow burn. It writes a **fast burn with pauses**. The characters are in love by chapter four; the tension is mechanical; the final kiss is a scheduled event.

Yarros does something genuinely different: she makes Violet and Xaden *despise* each other for a very long time, in a way that has actual stakes. Xaden's hostility is not a mask for attraction. It's a real grievance with a real history. When the shift begins, it begins because Violet has *seen* something in him that changes how she reads the earlier hostility — not because the plot needed a kiss.

That reframes every earlier scene. I reread the first third after finishing and found new tension in scenes I had read as simply hostile. That is the mark of a writer who knows what she is doing.

## Where it strains

The worldbuilding is where I get off the train.

Basgiath War College is supposedly the most selective military institution on the continent. It also has:

- Cadets who routinely murder each other in the halls with no consequences
- Instructors who seem to be on the side of whichever cadet will produce the best story beat
- A structure of *wings* and *squads* that feels imported from a modern university, not a military

This is fine as long as the book is honest that it is fantasy-of-manners with dragons. It is slightly less fine when it asks us to take the military setting seriously. Yarros mostly manages the trick by keeping the plot small and personal. When the series expands outward in *Iron Flame*, the strain becomes more visible.

## The dragons

They're good. Tairn and Andarna are the best-written creatures in the book — genuinely funny, genuinely alien, and willing to say the things to Violet that no human in her life will. The bond-as-a-three-way-conversation is one of the smartest structural choices Yarros makes: it lets her externalize interiority without interiority-heavy prose.

## Should you read it?

If you love romantasy and haven't read it: yes, absolutely. It's the moment. It's a full evening of your life. Read it with a friend so you can text each other at the reveals.

If you're sceptical of hype: yes, but calibrate. This is not a *fantasy* novel with romance in it. It is a **romance novel with dragons**, and it is excellent at being that. It is not trying to be *A Song of Ice and Fire* and it would fail if it were. Read it as a long-form love letter to enemies-to-lovers, and you'll have a great time.

**Content warnings:** graphic violence, death of major characters, chronic illness (own voice, handled with care), sexual content (explicit, late in the book).

★★★★☆
